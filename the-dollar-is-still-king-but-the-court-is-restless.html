The Dollar Is Still King — But the Court Is Restless

The dollar's dominance gives America a power no empire has ever possessed: the ability to weaponize the global financial system. That power is under more pressure than at any point since Bretton Woods.

By The Meridian Desk June 15, 2026 7 min read

Every time a Brazilian buys oil from a Saudi Arabian company, the transaction is settled in US dollars. Neither country uses dollars domestically. Neither country is American. But the dollar sits between them anyway — because that is how the global financial system has worked since 1944, and changing it is far harder than the people calling for change tend to admit.

The dollar's dominance is one of the most consequential and least understood features of the modern world. It gives the United States a power that no empire in history has possessed: the ability to weaponize the global financial system itself. And it is under more pressure than at any point since Bretton Woods created it.

✦ ✦ ✦

How the Dollar Became King

In July 1944, representatives of 44 Allied nations gathered at Bretton Woods, New Hampshire. The conference was dominated by two men: John Maynard Keynes, representing Britain, and Harry Dexter White, representing the United States. Keynes proposed a neutral international currency — the "bancor." White proposed the dollar. The United States was the world's largest economy, its largest creditor, and the only major power whose industrial base had not been bombed. White won.

In 1971, Nixon ended the gold convertibility promise. What replaced it was the petrodollar system: a 1974 agreement with Saudi Arabia that oil would be priced and sold exclusively in dollars, in exchange for American security guarantees. Every country that needed oil — which was every country — needed dollars. Global dollar demand was permanently secured.

What Reserve Currency Status Actually Means

Dollar dominance gives the United States extraordinary privileges. Because the world needs dollars, the US can borrow more cheaply than any other country. Because international trade runs through the dollar and American-controlled financial infrastructure, the US can exclude any country from the global economy by cutting off its dollar access. Sanctions on Iran, Russia, Venezuela, and North Korea all rely fundamentally on this. Without dollar dominance, they would be nearly toothless.

These privileges are enormous. They are also, increasingly, resented.

The De-Dollarization Push

The movement accelerated dramatically after 2022, when the United States froze approximately $300 billion in Russian central bank reserves held in Western institutions. This was unprecedented — central bank reserves had always been considered untouchable. The message to every non-Western government was clear: if you hold reserves in dollars, America can seize them.

China, which holds over $3 trillion in foreign exchange reserves, drew an obvious conclusion. Saudi Arabia began accepting yuan for some oil sales. BRICS expanded to include Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Egypt, with repeated discussion of a payment system bypassing the dollar. China and Russia increasingly settle trade in non-dollar currencies.

Why De-Dollarization Is Harder Than It Looks

The dollar accounts for roughly 58% of global foreign exchange reserves — still more than the euro, yuan, yen, and pound combined. About 90% of global foreign exchange transactions involve the dollar on one side. The reason is network effects: a reserve currency requires depth, liquidity, and trustworthiness that takes decades to build.

The yuan is not freely convertible — Beijing controls its exchange rate and restricts capital flows, making it fundamentally unsuitable as a global reserve currency. The euro's structural fragility was demonstrated by the eurozone debt crisis. Building an alternative requires not just the desire to escape dollar dominance but infrastructure that doesn't yet exist.

The Real Risk to the Dollar

The greatest threat to dollar dominance is not China or BRICS. It is the United States itself. America's national debt has passed $36 trillion. Its annual deficit runs in the trillions. The political system has repeatedly flirted with debt ceiling crises threatening default on US Treasury bonds — the supposedly risk-free asset the entire global financial system is built on. The dollar's dominance ultimately rests on trust. Every time that trust is shaken, the case for alternatives gets marginally stronger.

The Bottom Line

The dollar is not going to be dethroned soon. But dollar dominance peaked in the early 2000s and has been gradually declining. The weaponization of sanctions has accelerated the search for alternatives. The question is not whether dollar dominance will diminish but how quickly and what replaces it.

A world with multiple reserve currencies would be less efficient and more fragmented. It would also be one in which American power was significantly reduced. That transition, if it comes, will be one of the defining geopolitical shifts of the 21st century.
