Saudi Vision 2030: What MBS Is Actually Trying to Do

MBS promised to transform Saudi Arabia from an oil-dependent monarchy into a diversified modern economy. The progress is real, uneven, and often overstated — and it is being executed by a man who tolerates no dissent.

By The Meridian Desk June 11, 2026 7 min read

In 2016, a 30-year-old Saudi prince named Mohammed bin Salman announced that his country would transform itself. Oil would no longer be the foundation of the Saudi economy. Women would be allowed to drive, attend concerts, watch sports. A $500 billion futuristic city called NEOM would be built in the desert. Saudi Arabia would become a tourism destination, a technology hub, a cultural powerhouse. The plan was called Vision 2030.

Ten years on, Vision 2030 is simultaneously more impressive and more troubled than its architects will admit. To understand it properly, you have to understand not just what MBS is building — but why, what it costs, and what he is willing to do to protect it.

✦ ✦ ✦

The Problem Vision 2030 Is Trying to Solve

Saudi Arabia has one of the most dangerous economic structures of any country in the world: near-total dependence on a single commodity whose long-term demand is structurally declining. Oil accounts for roughly 70% of Saudi government revenue. The Saudi state uses that revenue to fund a social contract held together for decades — subsidized energy, free healthcare, free education, government jobs, and a standard of living that requires no taxation. In exchange, Saudis accept an absolute monarchy with no political rights.

This works when oil is expensive. It becomes unsustainable when oil is cheap, when global demand peaks, or when the wells run dry. The IEA projects global oil demand peaking sometime in the 2030s as electric vehicles penetrate the market. MBS identified this problem clearly. Vision 2030 is an attempt to solve it before it becomes a crisis.

What Has Actually Been Built

The social liberalization has moved fastest. Saudi Arabia issued its first tourist visas in 2019. It hosted Formula 1 races, boxing matches, and concerts by artists unimaginable in the kingdom five years earlier. Cinemas reopened after 35 years. Women gained the right to drive, attend sporting events, and travel without male guardian permission.

The Public Investment Fund has grown from $150 billion to over $700 billion, investing in Uber, Newcastle United, LIV Golf, AI companies, and real estate globally. NEOM — the $500 billion megaproject including a 170-kilometer linear city called The Line — has been repeatedly scaled back and has consumed enormous resources while producing, so far, primarily a construction site.

The Reforms and Their Limits

The social liberalization is real and carefully bounded. Women can drive, but Loujain al-Hathloul — one of the activists who campaigned for that right — was imprisoned and reportedly tortured. The right was granted; the people who fought for it were punished. Journalist Jamal Khashoggi was murdered inside the Saudi consulate in Istanbul in 2018 by Saudi operatives. A CIA assessment concluded MBS personally ordered the killing. He faced no meaningful international consequences.

The pattern is consistent: liberalization in the social and economic sphere, zero tolerance in the political sphere. Saudi citizens can watch Netflix and attend concerts. They cannot criticize the government.

The Geopolitical Repositioning

MBS has moved Saudi Arabia away from historical dependence on the United States. The kingdom joined BRICS. It coordinates oil production with Russia through OPEC+ despite American pressure. It normalized relations with Iran in a Chinese-brokered deal in 2023 — a stunning reversal after years of proxy conflict. The calculation is that America is a declining hegemon whose security guarantee is less reliable than it once was, and that Saudi Arabia's interests are better served by strategic autonomy.

Is It Working?

Partially, and not fast enough. The non-oil private sector has grown. Tourism revenues have increased. Entertainment has become a real industry. But oil still accounts for the majority of government revenue. Private sector jobs are not yet absorbing hundreds of thousands of young Saudis entering the workforce annually. NEOM has become a symbol of the gap between Vision 2030's ambition and its execution.

The Bottom Line

Vision 2030 is a serious attempt to solve a serious problem. But it is being executed by a man who tolerates no dissent, kills journalists, and has built a personal autocracy on top of a family monarchy. The economic transformation required is enormous — changing the fundamental structure of an oil-dependent rentier state is one of the hardest things a government can do.

MBS is simultaneously the most capable and most dangerous Saudi leader in modern history. Whether Vision 2030 succeeds or fails will determine not just the future of Saudi Arabia but the stability of the entire Gulf.
