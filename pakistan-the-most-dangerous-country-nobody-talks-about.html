Pakistan: The Most Dangerous Country Nobody Talks About

Pakistan has nuclear weapons, a military that runs the country regardless of civilian government, an economy in permanent crisis, and borders with Afghanistan, Iran, India, and China. It receives a fraction of the attention it deserves.

By The Meridian Desk July 1, 2026 7 min read

Pakistan has nuclear weapons. It has a military that runs the country regardless of which civilian government is nominally in power. It has an economy in permanent crisis, an IMF relationship measured in decades rather than years, and an intelligence service that has simultaneously fought terrorism and sponsored it depending on which group and which direction. It borders Afghanistan, Iran, India, and China. It is home to 240 million people.

It receives a fraction of the international attention given to smaller, less consequential crises. Pakistan is arguably the most fragile nuclear-armed state on earth, and its failure would be among the most destabilizing events of the 21st century.

✦ ✦ ✦

The Military's Country

Since Pakistan's founding in 1947, the military has directly ruled the country for roughly half of its existence through outright coups. When it is not directly ruling, it operates as a parallel government — controlling foreign policy, approving or vetoing civilian governments, managing the relationship with India, overseeing the nuclear program, and running a vast commercial empire. The ISI is effectively a state within the state.

Civilian governments in Pakistan exist at the military's sufferance. Zulfikar Ali Bhutto was hanged. Nawaz Sharif was imprisoned multiple times. Imran Khan was removed in 2022 when his relationship with the military broke down, then imprisoned in 2023 on charges widely seen as politically motivated.

The India Problem

Pakistan and India have fought four wars since their simultaneous independence in 1947. They have come close to nuclear exchange at least twice — during the 1999 Kargil conflict and the 2001-2002 military standoff. The partition of British India was one of history's most violent political events, killing between 200,000 and 2 million people and displacing 10-20 million. The trauma is foundational to Pakistani national identity in a way that almost no other founding event is not.

Kashmir was left unresolved. Both countries have claimed it since 1947. In 2019, India revoked Kashmir's special status, dividing it into two union territories — a move Pakistan condemned as illegal. Both countries have first-use nuclear doctrines, relatively short missile flight times between population centers, and a history of miscalculation that makes this the world's most dangerous bilateral relationship.

The Afghanistan Entanglement

The Pakistani military historically supported the Taliban as a strategic hedge against Indian influence in Afghanistan. The ISI ran networks of support for the Taliban through the 1990s and — according to extensive evidence — continued providing sanctuary during the post-2001 American occupation while simultaneously accepting American counterterrorism funding. Pakistan calculated, correctly, that it was too important to the American mission to be held fully accountable.

The Taliban's return to power in 2021 has not delivered the strategic benefits Pakistan expected. The monster it helped create has turned on its creator.

The Economic Spiral

Pakistan has been to the IMF 23 times since 1958 — a record representing not temporary difficulties but a structural inability to manage its finances. The fundamental problem is a state that spends far more than it collects, an elite that pays almost no taxes, a military that consumes an outsized share of the budget, and an export base too narrow to generate the foreign exchange needed to service external debt. Pakistan exports textiles and little else. Each IMF program imposes severe hardship on ordinary Pakistanis. The political consequences fuel instability. The instability undermines reforms. The cycle repeats.

The Nuclear Dimension

Pakistan has approximately 170 nuclear warheads, growing at roughly 10-15 per year. It has a tactical nuclear weapons program — small, battlefield-usable weapons designed to deter a conventional Indian advance — that has made the escalation ladder in any India-Pakistan conflict shorter and more dangerous. The A.Q. Khan network proliferated nuclear technology to North Korea, Libya, and Iran for decades with apparent institutional knowledge, demonstrating that transfers to non-state actors are not impossible.

The Bottom Line

Pakistan is not a failed state. It is a fragile state with nuclear weapons, a powerful military with conflicting interests, an economy in permanent crisis, and regional entanglements that pull it in incompatible directions simultaneously.

Pakistan gets less attention than it deserves because its crises are slow and chronic rather than sudden and acute. But chronic crises have tipping points. And Pakistan's margin for error, given what is at stake, is dangerously thin.
